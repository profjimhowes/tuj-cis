# The CS Baseline
### Prerequisites and meta-skills for the next four years

This is not study-tips advice. Every item below is something that either (a) gates your progress through the major, or (b) compounds — a fixed cost paid once that returns value every week for four years. Each section gives you a **self-test** (how to find out where you actually are), a **drill** (what to do about it), and a **threshold** (when to stop drilling and move on).

Nobody checks these for you. The degree does not require them. That is precisely why they separate students who finish from students who don't.

---

## 0. How to use this document

Run every self-test in the first two weeks of the semester. Write down the results. You will be tempted to skip the ones you expect to fail — those are the ones that matter. Re-run the whole battery at the start of each subsequent semester; the sharpening never finishes, but the drilling should get shorter each time.

Be honest in a specific way: the question is never "do I know this?" but "can I produce it, cold, under time pressure, without looking it up?" The gap between recognition and production is where most students lose four years.

## 1. Typing

**Why it's here, honestly.** There is no good evidence that typing speed *causes* better programming. Thinking is the bottleneck, not keystrokes. The honest case is narrower and still strong: slow typing taxes every *iteration* you make, and the tax is heaviest exactly when you're exploring — trying six variations of a loop, rewriting a function three ways, taking notes while someone talks. If typing costs you conscious attention, it is stealing attention from the problem. It also shows up directly in timed exams and in any interview where you code live. Jeff Atwood's ["We Are Typists First, Programmers Second"](https://blog.codinghorror.com/we-are-typists-first-programmers-second/) is the canonical argument; treat it as an opinion piece, not evidence, and evaluate it against your own experience.

**Self-test.** Go to [monkeytype.com](https://monkeytype.com), 60-second English test, three runs, take the median. Then go to [typing.io](https://typing.io) and do one run on real source code. Record both numbers. The second will be roughly half the first, and that gap is the interesting part — it measures your fluency with brackets, underscores, semicolons, and the number row.

**Threshold.**

| Prose WPM | What it means |
|---|---|
| < 40 | A genuine drag. Fix this now; it will pay back within one semester. |
| 40–60 | Functional. Worth pushing up if you're not touch-typing. |
| 60–80 | Fine. Stop optimizing WPM; optimize editing (§ below). |
| > 80 | You are not the bottleneck. Move on. |

Two conditions matter more than the number. **Are you touch-typing** — hands on home row, eyes never leaving the screen? If you look down, you are paying a context-switch cost on every glance regardless of your WPM. **Is your accuracy above 95%?** Speed with 88% accuracy is slower than it looks once you count backspaces.

**Drill.** Fifteen minutes a day, not sixty minutes a week. [keybr.com](https://keybr.com) is the right tool for building touch-typing because it adapts — it generates pseudo-words weighted toward the letters you're currently weakest on, rather than making you retype the same passage. Once you're touch-typing cleanly, switch to [typing.io](https://typing.io) or [speedcoder.net](https://www.speedcoder.net) for symbol fluency. Expect six to eight weeks to rebuild the habit if you're currently a hunt-and-peck typist, and expect your speed to *drop* for the first two weeks. That drop is the sign it's working.

**The thing that actually matters more than WPM: editing speed.** Watch a senior engineer and you'll notice they almost never touch the mouse and almost never use arrow keys one press at a time. Learn, in this order: word-wise and line-wise cursor movement, select-to-end-of-line, delete-word-backward, jump-to-line-number, jump-to-symbol/definition, multi-cursor, find-and-replace with regex, and your editor's file switcher. In the shell specifically: `Ctrl-A`, `Ctrl-E`, `Ctrl-W`, `Ctrl-K`, `Ctrl-U`, and `Ctrl-R` (reverse history search — the single highest-value keystroke in this document). Make a list of the ten mouse actions you perform most often and find the keyboard equivalent for each. One per day for two weeks.

## 2. Your hardware

You will be told you need an expensive machine. You mostly don't — but you do need to know exactly what your machine can and cannot do, because discovering the limit at 2 a.m. before a deadline is expensive.

**Self-test.** Answer these without looking anything up: How much RAM do you have? Is your storage SSD or spinning disk, and how much free space is on it? Is your CPU x86-64 or ARM? How much battery life do you actually get under load? Where do your files live when you save them, physically, on which drive? If you missed more than one, you don't know your machine.

**Threshold.** 8 GB of RAM and an SSD is a workable floor for everything in this degree. 16 GB is comfortable and is the single best upgrade if you're buying. 4 GB will make an IDE plus a browser plus a container genuinely painful — not impossible, but you'll need to work in a terminal editor and close things aggressively. Spinning disks are the worst remaining bottleneck in a student machine; if you have one and can replace it, do that before you upgrade anything else.

**The ARM trap, specifically.** If you have an Apple Silicon Mac (M1 through M4), you have an ARM processor. This is fine for almost everything and will bite you in **CIS 2107**, where you write x86-64 assembly. Your machine does not natively execute those instructions. Your options are a virtual machine with x86 emulation (slow but workable), a remote Linux host, or the department's machines. Find out which one the course expects *before* the semester starts, not during week 3.

**Working with what you've got.** If your local machine is weak, the answer is remote compute, and most of it is free to you. The [GitHub Student Developer Pack](https://education.github.com/pack) is the first thing to sign up for — it includes free GitHub Pro, [Codespaces](https://github.com/features/codespaces) hours (a full Linux dev environment in a browser tab), and a pile of other services. [JetBrains](https://www.jetbrains.com/community/education/) gives students free licenses for its entire IDE suite. Learn to `ssh` into a machine and work there; a Chromebook plus a remote host is a completely viable setup, and it happens to force you into shell fluency, which is a benefit disguised as a constraint.

**Backups, before you need them.** Some percentage of you will lose work this year. The rule is 3-2-1: three copies, two different media, one off-site. In practice: your working copy, an automated cloud sync (OneDrive/Drive/iCloud — Temple gives you storage), and `git push` to a remote. Note that git alone is not a backup if you never push, and cloud sync alone is not a backup if it faithfully syncs your deletion. Test your restore path once, deliberately.

**Cheap wins in Tokyo.** A second monitor and an external mechanical keyboard cost less than you think secondhand — Hard-Off, Akihabara's used-equipment floors, and Mercari all clear this stuff constantly. An external monitor is the highest-return small purchase in this list.

## 3. Computer operation and file organization

This section exists because of a real and measurable generational shift: students raised on search-based and app-based interfaces frequently do not have a working mental model of a hierarchical filesystem. If you've mostly found files by typing their name into a search box, you have a gap here, and it is not your fault, and it will absolutely wreck you in a compilers-and-paths world. Monica Chin's *File Not Found* (The Verge, 2021) documented professors discovering exactly this; it's worth reading to know whether you're describing yourself.

**Self-test.** Without using a search box: create a directory three levels deep, put a text file at the bottom, and write out its absolute path from memory. Then answer — what is your home directory's absolute path? What's the difference between `./notes.txt` and `~/notes.txt` and `/notes.txt`? What is the actual difference between a `.txt` and a `.c` file? What happens to a Java file if you rename it in the file browser? If your `Downloads` folder has more than 200 items in it, that's a finding too.

**Threshold.** You should be able to explain, unprompted: absolute vs. relative paths, what `.` and `..` and `~` mean, what a file extension does and does not do, the difference between text and binary files, what a hidden file is and how to see them, and roughly where your OS keeps user data versus program data.

**Habits worth adopting now.**

- One directory per project, one project per repository, everything under a single `~/code` or `~/dev` tree. Not on the Desktop, not in Downloads.
- No spaces in filenames for anything you'll touch from a shell. Use hyphens or underscores. You will thank yourself the first time you write a shell script.
- Know that `Untitled_final_v2_REAL_final.docx` is a symptom. Version control replaces this, and that's most of what version control is for.
- Learn what a text encoding is and set everything to UTF-8. Learn what a line ending is (`LF` on Unix, `CRLF` on Windows) and configure git's `core.autocrlf` correctly once. This will save you an inexplicable four-hour debugging session at some point.

## 4. Shell / terminal

Of everything on this list, shell fluency has the highest ratio of "obviously valuable in retrospect" to "actually taught anywhere in the curriculum." The department will assume you have it by CIS 2107 and will never have taught it to you.

**Self-test.** In a single command line each, no scripts:

1. Find every `.c` file under the current directory modified in the last seven days that contains the string `malloc`, and count them.
2. Show the ten largest files in a directory tree, sorted.
3. Take the output of a program, keep only lines matching a pattern, and write them to a file while still seeing them on screen.
4. Find out what the `-n` flag does for `sort`, without leaving the terminal and without a search engine.
5. Run a long job, background it, do something else, then bring it back to the foreground.
6. Recall a command you ran forty commands ago without pressing the up arrow forty times.

If you can't do four of these, you have a real gap. This is normal in a first-year student and is not normal in a third-year student.

**Threshold.** Comfortable, without looking up syntax, with: navigation and globbing; `grep`, `find`, `xargs`, `sed` at a basic level; pipes and all three redirection forms; `man` and `--help`; `chmod` and the permission bits; `ssh` and `scp`; `tar` and `zip`; job control (`&`, `Ctrl-Z`, `fg`, `bg`, `jobs`, `kill`); environment variables and `$PATH`; and what a dotfile is and where yours live.

**Drill and resources.** MIT's [The Missing Semester of Your CS Education](https://missingsemester.mit.edu/) is exactly this course's missing half — eleven lectures covering shell, scripting, editors, version control, debugging, and profiling. It is free, it is excellent, and working through it over one semester puts you meaningfully ahead of your cohort. Supplement with [explainshell.com](https://explainshell.com), which decomposes any command line into per-flag explanations; [ShellCheck](https://www.shellcheck.net) for finding bugs in scripts you write; and [Julia Evans' zines and blog](https://jvns.ca) for the best plain-language explanations of Unix internals available anywhere.

The drill itself is simple and unpleasant: for one month, do things in the shell that you would normally do in a GUI. Copy files, rename in bulk, search inside documents, unzip things. It will be slower for two weeks and then it won't be.

## 5. Mathematics, brutally

**Why this section is not optional.** Look at the actual prerequisite graph in your catalog. **CIS 2168** (Data Structures) requires precalculus. **CIS 2033** and **CIS 2166** require Calculus I. **CIS 3223** requires Calculus II *and* 2166 *and* 2033 — and 3296 requires 3207, which requires 2168. Math is not a parallel track that you can fall behind in. It is a serial dependency in the middle of your degree, and every semester you slip in math is a semester you slip in CS, compounding. Students who arrive needing remedial algebra and don't attack it immediately routinely graduate a year late, and almost none of them saw it coming in their first semester.

**Self-test, and it has to be real.** Your high-school grades are not evidence; they measure a different thing under different conditions. Take an actual placement diagnostic and accept the result. [Math Academy](https://mathacademy.com) has a diagnostic that is unusually good at finding the specific missing prerequisites rather than just assigning a grade level. Khan Academy's course challenges are a free alternative. Whatever you use, do it cold, untimed if you like, but without help.

The gaps that actually kill CS students are rarely "advanced" topics. They are: fluency with algebraic manipulation (can you simplify a rational expression without thinking about it?), logarithms and exponents (you will meet these on day one of complexity analysis), function notation and composition, summation and product notation, inequalities, and basic logic — implication, contrapositive, quantifiers. **CIS 1166** will ask you to write proofs, and the students who struggle there are almost never struggling with proof technique; they're struggling because the algebra underneath isn't automatic and it's consuming all their working memory.

**Threshold.** The test is not "can I solve this" but "can I solve this *without conscious effort*." Automaticity is the goal, and it is not a lesser goal than understanding — it is the precondition for it. You cannot reason about an algorithm's growth rate while simultaneously working out how to combine logarithms. Working memory is small and fixed; fluency is how you buy room in it.

**Drill.** Re-reading and re-watching do not work for this, and the reason they feel like they work is exactly the problem (see §7). What works is spaced retrieval practice on problems, at volume, over months. [Math Academy](https://mathacademy.com) is built explicitly on this model — mastery-based, spaced, with prerequisite-aware sequencing — and Justin Skycak's [writing on the underlying learning science](https://www.justinmath.com/) is worth reading on its own terms even if you don't use the product. If you're paying for one thing on this list, this is the one with the clearest return.

## 6. Spaced repetition and memorization

**The claim.** You have probably been told that memorization is the low-value opposite of real understanding. This is backwards. Memorization and automaticity are *prerequisites* for higher-order reasoning, not substitutes for it. Everything you have to consciously look up is occupying space you could be using to think. The reason experts appear to reason better is substantially that they've offloaded the basics into recall and freed the machinery.

**The evidence.** Two robust findings, both a century old and repeatedly replicated. The **testing effect**: retrieving information from memory strengthens retention far more than re-studying it does — Roediger & Karpicke, *Test-Enhanced Learning* (Psychological Science, 2006), is the standard citation. The **spacing effect**: the same total study time produces dramatically better long-term retention when distributed across days rather than massed — see Cepeda et al.'s 2006 meta-analysis in *Psychological Bulletin*, covering 254 studies. Spaced repetition software is simply the mechanization of both.

**The tool.** [Anki](https://apps.ankiweb.net/) is free (except on iOS), open source, and the default. Its modern scheduler (FSRS) is genuinely good. The learning curve is one evening.

**What to card, in CS specifically.** Not concepts, and not code. Card the things where lookup latency is the cost: shell flags and command syntax, editor keybindings, Java Collections API signatures and their complexity guarantees, C standard library function signatures (`strncpy`'s argument order will bite you), operator precedence, git commands, math identities, and vocabulary — the field is dense with terms whose meanings you must have instantly.

**How to card, which matters more than whether.** Make your own cards; shared decks mostly don't work because the encoding happens during authoring. Keep cards atomic — one fact, one card. Write the prompt so that it can only be answered one way. Never card something you don't already understand; SRS maintains knowledge, it doesn't create it. The three references worth reading before you start are Michael Nielsen's [Augmenting Long-term Memory](https://augmentingcognition.com/ltm.html), Andy Matuschak's [How to write good prompts](https://andymatuschak.org/prompts/), and Wozniak's [Twenty rules of formulating knowledge](https://www.supermemo.com/en/blog/twenty-rules-of-formulating-knowledge). Budget an hour for all three; it's the difference between a deck that works and a deck you abandon in five weeks.

**Threshold.** Ten to fifteen minutes daily, every day, no exceptions — the schedule collapses if you skip, and the debt compounds visibly. If your daily review is exceeding twenty minutes, you're adding too many cards, not reviewing too slowly.

## 7. AI: what helps and what quietly destroys you

This is the most consequential section here and the one where your instincts will be worst, because the harmful uses feel *better* while you're doing them.

**The mechanism.** Learning happens through effortful retrieval and generation. When you struggle to produce something and eventually produce it, you encode it. When you read a fluent explanation of it instead, you experience the feeling of understanding without the encoding — and that feeling is not a reliable signal. This is well-documented independent of AI: Deslauriers et al. (PNAS, 2019) found that students in active-learning physics classes *learned more but felt they learned less* than students in fluent lectures, and preferred the lectures. Fluency feels like competence. It isn't. An LLM is the most fluent explanation-generator ever built, which makes it the most effective illusion-of-competence generator ever built.

**The evidence, specifically.** Bastani et al., *Generative AI Can Harm Learning* (2024), ran a randomized controlled trial with roughly a thousand high-school math students in Turkey, across four sessions of assisted practice followed by unassisted exams. <cite index="6-1">Students with access to a ChatGPT-like interface improved 48% on practice problems, and those with a specially safeguarded tutor improved 127%</cite>. Then the tools were taken away for the exam. <cite index="4-1">The students who had learned with the unrestricted ChatGPT interface scored 17% worse on the AI-free exams than students who had never had access at all.</cite> <cite index="2-1">The authors attribute this to students using the model as a crutch and being unwilling to spend the effort to check its correctness; the safeguarded tutor largely mitigated the harm.</cite> [[Full paper]](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/64398599b0c21f1705fb8fb3/t/66c7e970ae81b81a53295abc/1724377456931/ssrn-4895486+(4).pdf) [[Summary]](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/without-guardrails-generative-ai-can-harm-education/)

Read that result carefully, because it is not "AI is bad." It is: **the same tool made performance go up and learning go down, simultaneously, and the students could not tell.** The difference between the two arms was entirely in how the tool was configured to interact. Since nobody is going to configure it for you, you have to impose the safeguards yourself.

**The single test that resolves every case.** *Can I reproduce this tomorrow, from a blank editor, with no assistance?* If no, you didn't learn it — you watched it. Apply the test literally: close the tab, wait a day, try to rebuild it. The discomfort of failing this test is the most useful information you will get all semester.

**Uses that help.**

- **Attempt first, always.** Write your own version, get stuck, articulate *specifically* what you're stuck on, and only then ask. The articulation alone solves maybe a third of them.
- **Make it examine you, not explain to you.** "Quiz me on pointer arithmetic, one question at a time, don't give me the answer until I've tried, and tell me what my wrong answer reveals about my misconception." This is the single highest-value prompt pattern in this document.
- **Generate practice, not solutions.** "Give me eight problems of increasing difficulty on linked-list manipulation." Volume of graded practice is exactly what education under-supplies and what a model supplies cheaply.
- **Explain it back and get corrected.** Write your understanding in your own words, paste it, and ask what's wrong or missing. This is retrieval practice with an automated grader.
- **Error messages, after you've read them.** Read it yourself first, form a hypothesis, then ask. Reading compiler and linker errors is a learnable skill that you will never acquire if you paste them reflexively.
- **Unfamiliar codebases and documentation.** Legitimately excellent at "what does this file do, in one paragraph."

**Uses that damage you.**

- **Autocomplete during the learning phase.** Copilot-style inline completion is very good and, while you are acquiring syntax and idiom, it removes exactly the generation step that builds them. Turn it off in CIS 1057, 1068, and 2168. Turn it back on later when the fundamentals are automatic.
- **Pasting the assignment prompt.** Beyond the academic-integrity problem, this replaces the entire learning event with a transcription exercise.
- **Asking for code before you have a specification.** If you can't state precisely what the function should do, you can't evaluate what you get back — and you will accept it because it looks plausible.
- **Accepting explanations you can't reconstruct.** The comfortable feeling at the end of a good explanation is not evidence.
- **Debugging by re-prompting.** Pasting the error back repeatedly until something compiles produces code you don't understand and no debugging skill. Debugging is hypothesis-driven: form a theory, design a test that discriminates, run it, narrow.

**One thing worth internalizing about your position.** These tools are best at exactly the work that entry-level engineers used to be hired to do. The work that remains is judgment: deciding what to build, evaluating whether an output is correct, designing abstractions, and knowing enough about the layer below to diagnose what breaks. Every one of those requires the deep knowledge that skipping the struggle prevents you from acquiring. Your degree is worth what your judgment is worth.

## 8. Reading and debugging

**Reading documentation** is a distinct skill from reading tutorials, and the transition is a real threshold. Tutorials are narrative and tell you what to do; references are structured and tell you what is true. Practice: answer a question using only `man` pages, or only the Java API docs, or only the C standard — no search engine, no tutorial, no model. Do this deliberately once a week. The specific sub-skills are knowing a reference page's standard sections, reading a function signature and inferring usage from it, and finding the one paragraph in a spec that resolves your question.

**Debugging** is the skill most correlated with actual productivity and the one least taught. The discipline: reproduce reliably, reduce to a minimal example, form a hypothesis that predicts something *specific*, design an observation that would falsify it, and narrow. Learn an actual debugger — `gdb` for C, your IDE's debugger for Java — to the level of breakpoints, stepping, watches, and inspecting the stack. `printf` debugging is legitimate and insufficient. Learn to write a minimal reproducible example; [Stack Overflow's guide](https://stackoverflow.com/help/minimal-reproducible-example) is the standard reference, and the discipline of producing one solves your bug outright often enough to be worth it even when nobody reads it.

**Version control** is assumed and rarely taught. The floor: commit early and often with messages that explain *why*, branch and merge, resolve a conflict without panicking, read a `diff`, use `git log` and `git blame` to answer questions about history, and recover from a mistake with `reflog`. Understand that git is a content-addressed graph of snapshots — the commands make sense only once that model is in place, and stay permanently confusing until it is. [Pro Git](https://git-scm.com/book/en/v2) is free and is the book.

## 9. Career, starting now

**The compounding-presence habit.** The highest-return career habit is unglamorous: go to office hours every week with one specific prepared question, for one course, all semester. Not when you're in trouble — every week. By the end of the semester a faculty member knows your name and your work, and that is the mechanism by which research positions, recommendation letters, and internship referrals actually get allocated. **CIS 4397** (Independent Research) requires a 3.0 overall and 3.3 in-major GPA and a faculty supervisor who has agreed to work with you; the GPA gate you handle in coursework, and the supervisor gate you handle by having been a known quantity for two years.

The same logic applies to everything else: attend the talks, join the club, go to the meetup. Low-variance showing-up beats high-intensity bursts, and most opportunity arrives through weak ties rather than close friends — Granovetter's *The Strength of Weak Ties* (1973) is the source of that claim and it has held up across fifty years of replication.

**Two hiring calendars, and you must pick.** This is the part nobody will tell you, and it is specific to being at TUJ.

*US-style tech recruiting* is rolling and runs roughly nine to twelve months ahead. Summer internship applications open the previous **August–October**. It rewards a public portfolio, a résumé, and a demonstrable GitHub presence, and it is largely indifferent to your year as long as you can do the work.

*Japanese 新卒一括採用 (shinsotsu ikkatsu saiyō)* is a synchronized cohort process on a published national schedule. Information activities open around **March of your junior year**, selection around **June**, with informal offers (内々定) following; internships in the summer before that are effectively part of the screening. It rewards a different set of artifacts entirely — 自己分析, ES (エントリーシート), SPI testing, group discussion practice — and being late to it means being late by a full year, because the cycle doesn't repeat. Note that foreign-founded and English-first tech companies in Tokyo increasingly hire off-cycle on the US-style model, so the two tracks are not strictly geographic.

You do not need to choose your career in your first year. You do need to know, by the end of your sophomore year, which calendar you're on — because they demand preparation at different times and in different formats, and it is entirely possible to miss both by defaulting.

**Artifacts that compound.** Three finished projects with real READMEs beat thirty abandoned ones and beat a full contribution graph. "Finished" means someone else can clone it and run it from the README alone. Update your résumé at the end of every semester while you remember what you did, not in the panic month. Keep a running file of things you built and problems you solved; you will not remember them in two years, and interview answers come out of that file.

**Where the Tokyo tech community actually is.** [connpass.com](https://connpass.com/) is the aggregator for essentially all Japanese tech events and study groups (勉強会) — it is where things get announced, and it is not obvious to people arriving from outside Japan. [TokyoDev](https://www.tokyodev.com/) covers English-language software work in Japan, including job listings and honest writing about visas, contracts, and compensation. Both are free and both are worth checking monthly starting now, years before you need a job.
