# 0. How to approach this course

According to the course catalog:

> Students will solve programming problems using procedural programming constructs such as variable assignment, loops, branching structures, and functions. Students will write programs that are testable (using assertions) and maintainable (using good programming style, naming conventions, indentation, and comments). Topics covered also include the general characteristics of computers, techniques of problem solving, and algorithm specification. Students also begin the debugging and testing of computer programs in the C language. Students are also introduced to software engineering practices, including unit testing techniques, debugging techniques, and version control management.

Use the analogy of operators vs. engineers from Sorva's thesis to demonstrate that merely writing programs to solve specific puzzles isn't necessarily the best way to learn programming.

## 0.1 Mathematics background

Prerequisite is MATH 702 Intermediate Algebra, which has the following course description:

> This course covers the real number system, basic properties of real numbers, operations with fractional expressions, simplifying complex fractions, powers and roots, operations with radicals, graphing linear equations and inequalities, and factoring of polynomials.

Notably, that course is a prerequisite for MATH 1021 College Algebra:

> This course covers polynomial, rational and algebraic expressions, equations and inequalities. It also includes some topics in graphing, an introduction to the concept of a function, and a brief introduction to the exponential and logarithmic functions.

So CIS 1057 does not require the full college algebra course, which is apparently the first place where the concept of a function is introduced. Concerning.

## 1.1 Brief history of computers and programming

Computers are machines. Machines are powered devices which perform useful work. Humans have been designing and building ever-more complicated machines since the dawn of history, most of which are used to effect progressive physical or chemical transformations on raw input materials to turn them into finished products.

By the early 20th century, humans had already achieved a staggering level of complexity in the design of industrial machinery. Much of this complexity is a result of configurable and extensible design. That is, a single machine may be adjusted to perform a variety of different but related tasks, or connected to other machines in a standardized way in order to coordinate performance of larger tasks.

Adjustment and configuration of machinery is typically a manual process involving manipulation of various parts of the machine. We say that a machine is *programmable* when it can automatically perform these adjustments based on a sequence of externally provided symbolic instructions. The first programmable machine used in manufacturing was the Jacquard loom, patented in 1804, which used a sequence of punched cards to specify the precise pattern that should be woven into fabric.

- Babbage and Hollerith: punched cards for data storage and fast tabulation
- Konrad Zuse: programmable electromechanical computers
- Von Neumann: Early electronic computers and the stored-program paradigm
- From transistors to microprocessors and ISA standardization
- Backus and Ritchie: high-level languages and the dawn of modern software engineering
