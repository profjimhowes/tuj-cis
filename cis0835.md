# Overview

## Catalog description
This course provides students with an understanding of the underlying foundations of technology that have an impact on their lives and integrates those principles with real-world activities. It promotes information fluency by giving students an understanding of the foundational concepts from which the technology is derived and helping them to develop higher-level intellectual capabilities for applying the technology. The class is broken into three sections - technology principles, technology's impact in society, and labs. It is the combination of these three sections that will enable students to understand the foundation and the implementation of technology in their daily lives. Class lectures incorporating a myriad of digital technologies including computer hardware, readings, labs and student discussions will be utilized to integrate the application of technology and the impact facing society.

NOTE: (1) Duplicate Course: No credit for students who have taken CIS 1055 (C055), 1955 (H095) or 0935. (2) This course fulfills a Science & Technology (GS) requirement for students under GenEd and Science & Technology Second Level (SB) for students under Core.

## General Education

### About GenEd
GenEd is the *signature* curriculum for all Temple students.  Every Temple undergraduate student's education includes completion of the GenEd curriculum, with over 200 courses to choose from.

While majors and minors provide students a depth of knowledge in a specific field, the General Education curriculum provides students with the breadth of knowledge to make the major(s) and minor(s) work for them in a dynamic, constantly evolving world. The skills and goals built into GenEd classes describe habits of mind that are important not only in an academic context but are also sought after by employers and central to civic participation. The GenEd curriculum complements the major and guarantees that every Temple student, regardless of major, has space to explore.

### About GS
GenEd Science & Technology courses present scientific process in context, helping students understand how scientific phenomena and/or technological change affects human life and the planet.

Science & Technology courses are intended to teach students how to
- understand and describe the scientific process;
- understand the sequential nature of science and technology;
- recognize, use and appreciate scientific or technological thinking for solving problems that are part of everyday life;
- understand and communicate how technology encourages the process of discovery in science and related disciplines; and
- retrieve, organize, and analyze data associated with a scientific or technological model.

# Knowledge Map

## Motivation
An enumeration of the *irreducible* factual base for the course: the material that must reach automaticity before any bench or scenario work is intelligible. It is deliberately **not** the course outline. It is the substrate the outline stands on.

Two organizing axes:
- **Threads** (rows) — six accretion narratives, each of the form *constraint → response → new constraint*. These carry the GS "sequential nature of science and technology" bullet and give every otherwise-trivial fact a reason to exist.
- **Categories** (columns) — the five retrieval types. Each demands a different item format; conflating them is the main way a drill bank goes soft.

### The five categories

| Category | Question form | Failure if omitted |
|---|---|---|
| **V — Visual** | *Which of these is X?* (photo) | Verbal association without perceptual grounding; student cannot identify the part on a table |
| **N — Nomenclature** | *X ↔ abbreviation ↔ referent* | Cannot read documentation, error messages, or spec sheets |
| **S — Sequence** | *Which superseded which, and why?* | Facts stay trivia; no GS sequence bullet |
| **F — Function** | *What role does X play?* | Recognition without comprehension; bench work becomes ritual |
| **M — Magnitude** | *Is this value plausible?* | No model to check observations against; GS data bullet unmet |

**M is the highest-value and most-neglected column.** It is the one that directly serves "retrieve, organize, and analyze data associated with a technological model," and the one non-majors most conspicuously lack. Budget accordingly.

### The bounding filter

Keep an item if **either**:
1. it has a referent the student will physically encounter in their own life, **or**
2. it is load-bearing for one of the six threads.

Cut otherwise. Applied to A+ Core 1, this removes most of the printer domain, RAID levels beyond "redundancy exists," laptop disassembly specifics, SOHO configuration procedure, and essentially everything that exists only to close a ticket.

## 1. The six threads

### T1 — Compute
> **Constraint:** one general-purpose core, and clock frequency stalled around 2005 (end of Dennard scaling). **Response:** more cores. **New constraint:** most everyday workloads don't parallelize. **Response:** specialize the silicon — GPU, then NPU.

The thread with the most direct society payload: the reason AI became economically
feasible is a hardware story, not a software one.

### T2 — Memory
> **Constraint:** the CPU outruns DRAM (the memory wall). **Response:** cache hierarchy, then wider and faster buses (DDR generations). **New constraint:** bandwidth has grown by orders of magnitude while *latency* has barely moved — a superb magnitude lesson.

### T3 — Storage
> **Constraint:** mechanical seek and rotational latency. **Response:** flash. **New constraint:** flash inherits a protocol designed for spinning disks. **Response:** NVMe over PCIe. **New constraint:** cost and endurance per byte → tiering → cloud.

### T4 — Interconnect
> **Constraint:** parallel buses cannot scale (skew, pin count). **Response:** serial point-to-point lanes — PCIe, SATA, USB. **New constraint:** connector proliferation. **Response:** convergence on USB-C with alternate modes.

Students have personally lived the dongle era. Exploit that.

### T5 — Networking
> **Constraint:** a shared medium means collisions. **Response:** switching. **Then:** address exhaustion → NAT → IPv6. **Wireless branch:** spectrum contention → 802.11 generations, MIMO, 5/6 GHz.

### T6 — Power & thermal
> **Constraint:** every watt becomes heat in a fixed volume. **Response:** heatsinks, heat pipes, vapor chambers, then aggressive power management and heterogeneous cores. **Consequence:** performance is a thermal budget, which is why phones throttle.

*(Optional T7 — Display/human interface. Folded into T4 in this draft to hold the bounds.)*

## 2. The map

Counts are atomic facts, not PL variants. Expect 3–5 variants per fact.

### T1 — Compute (~35 items)

- **V** — CPU (LGA vs PGA underside), CPU socket with retention arm, IHS, discrete GPU card, laptop SoC in situ, motherboard chipset heatsink, integrated vs discrete GPU on a board, M.2 vs PCIe slot confusion pairs. *(~9)*
- **N** — CPU, GPU, NPU, SoC, core, thread, clock/GHz, socket, chipset, integrated graphics, x86 vs ARM, 32- vs 64-bit. *(~12)*
- **S** — single core → multicore → heterogeneous (P/E) → accelerators. x86 desktop → ARM mobile → ARM desktop. *(~5)*
- **F** — CPU as general-purpose sequencer vs GPU as wide parallel array; what a chipset brokers; why "more cores" doesn't help most tasks. *(~5)*
- **M** — clock 3–5 GHz and flat for 20 years; core counts 4–16 consumer; GPU cores in the thousands; a plausible-vs-implausible spec-sheet sort. *(~4)*

### T2 — Memory (~35 items)

- **V** — DIMM vs SODIMM, DDR3/4/5 notch positions (near-miss set), ECC module's extra chip, heat spreader, populated vs empty slots, soldered LPDDR on a laptop board. *(~8)*
- **N** — RAM, DRAM, volatile, DIMM, SODIMM, ECC, channel, DDR, MT/s vs MHz, form factor. *(~10)*
- **S** — DDR1→DDR5: transfer rate doubling, voltage dropping, notch moving (which is *why* they're mutually incompatible — a physical answer to "why can't I just use my old RAM"). *(~6)*
- **F** — **memory vs storage** — the single most important misconception to break for non-majors ("my phone is out of memory"); working set; why more RAM stops helping. *(~6)*
- **M** — the latency ladder: L1 ~1 ns, L3 ~15 ns, DRAM ~80 ns, NVMe ~20 µs, HDD ~5 ms, transpacific RTT ~120 ms. Capacities 8/16/32/64 GB. *(~5)*

**Note:** the latency ladder is arguably the highest-yield single object in the whole map. It's one table, it spans every thread, and it makes orders of magnitude viscerally real. Consider drilling it to fluency early and referring back to it all semester.

### T3 — Storage (~40 items)

- **V** — 3.5" HDD, 2.5" SATA SSD, M.2 2280, **M.2 B-key vs M-key notch (SATA vs NVMe)**, SD/microSD, USB flash drive, SATA data vs SATA power connector, eMMC soldered in situ, optical drive. *(~11)*
- **N** — HDD, SSD, NVMe, SATA, M.2, platter, RPM, controller, wear/endurance, form factor, GB vs TB, cloud storage. *(~12)*
- **S** — floppy → optical → flash → cloud; HDD → SATA SSD → NVMe; *why* the SATA interface became the bottleneck once the mechanism disappeared. *(~7)*
- **F** — persistence; what a controller does; why deleting isn't erasing (society hook: data remanence, resale, disposal). *(~6)*
- **M** — seek 5–10 ms vs 0.1 ms vs 0.02 ms; throughput 150 / 550 / 3500–7000 MB/s; "how long to copy 50 GB over each?" *(~4)*

### T4 — Interconnect (~55 items — the largest bucket, and the most everyday-relevant)

- **V** — USB-A, USB-B, micro-USB, mini-USB, USB-C, Lightning, HDMI, mini-HDMI, DisplayPort, mini-DP, VGA, DVI, RJ45, RJ11, 3.5 mm TRS, Thunderbolt marking, PCIe x1/x4/x16 slots, SATA port, 24-pin ATX, 8-pin EPS, 6+2-pin PCIe power, barrel jack. *(~22)*
- **N** — **port vs connector vs cable vs protocol** (a conceptual distinction, drilled as nomenclature); alternate mode; bus; lane; host vs device; upstream/downstream. *(~10)*
- **S** — parallel → serial; VGA → DVI → HDMI → DisplayPort; USB 1/2/3/4 *and the naming disaster* (a clean case study in marketing overwriting engineering — worth its own discussion session). *(~10)*
- **F** — one cable carrying power + data + display; why a USB-C cable that charges may not drive a monitor (the everyday failure students have all hit). *(~7)*
- **M** — USB 2.0 480 Mb/s, 3.0 5 Gb/s, USB4 40 Gb/s; HDMI version vs 4K120 feasibility; PCIe lanes × generation → GB/s. **"Can this cable carry this signal?"** is the model-check item type — build many. *(~6)*

### T5 — Networking (~50 items)

- **V** — consumer combo box, standalone switch, access point, cable modem, ONT, RJ45 vs RJ11 near-miss, coax F-connector, fiber LC/SC, external antenna. *(~9)*
- **N** — IP address, MAC address, DNS, DHCP, NAT, SSID, LAN/WAN, packet, **bandwidth vs throughput vs latency** (three words students use interchangeably), router, switch, modem, firewall, VPN. *(~14)*
- **S** — hub → switch (collision domains); IPv4 exhaustion → NAT → IPv6; 802.11 b/g/n/ac/ax/be → the Wi-Fi 4/5/6/7 rebranding. *(~10)*
- **F** — why your home router is four devices in one box; what DNS actually does (society hook: censorship, blocking, resolution as a control point); NAT as accidental firewall. *(~9)*
- **M** — advertised vs achieved Wi-Fi throughput; RTT local ~1 ms, domestic ~20 ms, **Tokyo→US-East ~120–160 ms**; speed of light in fiber as a hard physical floor no money can buy past. *(~8)*

**The latency floor is the best single GS moment in the course** and it's free to you here: students in Japan feel transpacific latency daily, and the explanation bottoms out in a physical constant. Problem identification, model, prediction, measurement, comparison.

### T6 — Power & thermal (~30 items)
- **V** — PSU, modular vs non-modular cabling, tower heatsink, heat pipe, laptop vapor chamber, blower vs axial fan, thermal paste, laptop battery pack, UPS. *(~9)*
- **N** — watt, volt, amp, DC vs AC, TDP, throttling, PSU wattage rating, Wh, surge protector vs UPS. *(~9)*
- **S** — frequency scaling era → multicore → heterogeneous cores → mobile SoC power management. *(~4)*
- **F** — every watt becomes heat; sustained vs burst performance; why the same chip is faster in a laptop than a phone. *(~4)*
- **M** — phone ~5 W, laptop 30–65 W, desktop GPU 300–450 W, datacenter rack ~10–20 kW; battery Wh vs runtime. *(~4)*
