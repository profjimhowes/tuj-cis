### Achieving "A's for All (as Time and Interest Allow)"
Workshop from ACM Learning @ Scale (L@S) 2022
https://scispace.com/pdf/achieving-a-s-for-all-as-time-and-interest-allow-kzlostqc.pdf

The SIGCSE-MEMBERS mailing list of the ACM Special Interest Group in Computer Science Education is the main forum for educators worldwide to discuss computing education research, pedagogy, and curriculum. In early 2022 it was abuzz with several connected movements: growth mindset, proficiency (aka mastery) learning, grading for equity, and specifications grading. Each of these is an important step toward the Holy Grail: A’s for All (as time and interest allow); the “A” line doesn’t move, but every student should be given an opportunity to achieve proficiency and earn it, as long as they are willing to put in the time and effort it might take. The mantra is not “fixed time, variable learning”, but “fixed learning, variable time”. The goal of this full-day workshop is to provide educators and administrators with tools to achieve it in their courses and institutions.

One useful analogy is that enrolling in a course is like taking a trip by bus: there is one speed for everyone, it lasts 10 or 15 weeks, and at the end of the trip a calculation is made regarding what percentage of the pile of points has been earned, and an A-F grade is assigned. What happens when someone needs (or wants) to travel slower, and as a result will get off the bus? Do they wait a whole term for the next bus? Can they work on their own? Do they get on the "local" bus that goes slower, perhaps with a supportive cohort who also needed to go slower? This is typically not allowed by our *assembly-line* model of learning, yet there are many ways that an institution can support these students, which we will address.

### A Multi-Institutional Study on Peer Instruction: Evaluating Text-Chat with Assigned Group Members vs Verbal Discussion
Accepted to ACM SIGCSE TS 2026
> [!abstract]-
> In Peer Instruction (PI) an instructor displays a hard multiple-choice question during lecture that students answer individually, discuss verbally with nearby peers, answer individually again, and finally, the instructor leads a discussion of the question. Peer Instruction typically increases student learning and motivation over traditional lecture. We added a text-chat mode to improve PI for remote synchronous learning. This feature assigns students to discussion groups to maximize the number of groups that have members with different answers. The tool was pilot tested in Winter 2022 and revised. In Fall 2022 and Winter 2023 it was tested at one institution. In Fall 2024 it was tested at four institutions. We conducted a log file analysis of student data from 1394 students and analyzed surveys with 848 student responses. We found that questions answered using the text-chat had a significantly higher improvement than those using traditional verbal discussion, although the two modes were tested with different questions. Interestingly, most of the students preferred to discuss the question verbally, although some preferred the text-chat discussion. These results inform efforts to improve the effectiveness of Peer Instruction and increase its adoption.

### Designing and Implementing Skill Tests at Scale: Frequent, Computer-Based, Proctored Assessments with Minimal Infrastructure Requirements
Accepted to ACM SIGCSE TS 2026
> [!abstract]-
> The rise of Large Language Models has intensified the need for reliable assessments of foundational programming skills in introductory computer science courses. While frequent, low-stakes testing is an effective pedagogical strategy, its adoption is often slowed by the need for institutional infrastructure like a Computer-Based Testing Facility, which instructors may lack or find too inflexible. This experience report presents a practical, instructor-driven model for Skill Tests: weekly, 10-minute, proctored, computer-based assessments run by course staff in any campus room using student laptops. We provide a logistical blueprint refined over five offerings of a large CS1 course, detailing our strategies for question design, student scheduling, and staff management. Our findings show this model is effective: replacing a midterm with Skill Tests reduced student anxiety, with 91% of students preferring the new format. Student performance on the final exam remained consistent, indicating that knowledge synthesis was not compromised. This paper offers educators a framework for deploying frequent, low-stakes assessments without dedicated institutional resources.

### Enabling Open Educational Resource Adoption through Integrated Sharing in PrairieLearn
Accepted to ACM SIGCSE TS 2026
> [!abstract]-
> This tool paper introduces the PrairieLearn Question Sharing System (PQSS), which enables instructors to share questions and question generators with other instructors, either as open educational resources or privately. PQSS is directly integrated into PrairieLearn, an open- source, problem-driven online learning platform. PQSS addresses a critical need for more open-source assessments by making it easier for instructors to share assessments they’ve created and for instructors to use those assessments. Instructors often don’t share questions that they have created due to the time it takes to publish them and the lack of recognition for their work. Because it is directly integrated into PrairieLearn, PQSS reduces the aforementioned friction of sharing and using shared questions, and we can report usage statistics to help question authors receive recognition for their work. In this paper, we share design and implementation details of the system, as well as experiences using it to share course content across courses and between universities.

### Encouraging Learning Through Repetition: Effects of Multiple Practice Opportunities in a Large Intro Programming Course
Accepted to ACM SIGCSE TS 2026
> [!abstract]-
> It is well established that repetition, especially when spaced over multiple sleep cycles, significantly enhances learning and long-term retention. However, introductory computer science (CS) courses present a unique pedagogical challenge: students enter with vastly different levels of prior experience. For some, the material is entirely new, offering no opportunity for repetition; for others, it is largely review. Traditional instructional models tend to privilege those who grasp content on the first pass, while offering little structural support for students who need more time. Meanwhile, more equitable strategies like mastery-based grading are difficult to scale in large-enrollment settings due to resource constraints. In response to these challenges, we designed a lesson structure that builds repetition into the course flow without penalizing students for slower initial understanding and without increasing workload for educators. Our solution is a weekly workbook composed of four components: a conceptual overview, followed by three linked assignments. These assignments progress in emphasis from assignment completion (to build confidence, familiarity, and provide early feedback) to correctness (to support mastery). Early results suggest improved retention and higher performance among our students. This model encourages re-engagement with material across multiple contexts and time points, giving all students – regardless of background – space to improve through repetition while still balancing the resource demand of a large foundational class.

### Enhancing Student Engagement and Learning in Database Programming Through Active Learning Strategies
Accepted to ACM SIGCSE TS 2026
> [!abstract]-
> This paper presents an educational intervention designed to promote students’ engagement, interest, and active involvement, while enriching their learning experience, in a required undergraduate course on database programming. The intervention is structured around a framework that integrates three active learning strategies: (1) think-pair-share to address theoretical concepts, while enhancing conceptual understanding, critical thinking, and participation; (2) gamification with program-building cards, to address algorithmic aspects, while boosting group collaboration and peer-interaction; and (3) peer-assessment to evaluate final programs, while encouraging students’ reflection and critical analysis of their learning. The framework’s effectiveness was assessed through a comparative analysis of student performance between experimental and control groups, feedback from the experimental group, and instructor’s perceptions. Results showed that students in the experimental group significantly outperformed their peers on examination averages. They also reported higher motivation and engagement in their learning while performing the framework’s activities. The instructor observed that these students felt more motivated and actively involved when participating in the framework. Given its potential applicability to other courses, the paper concludes with lessons learned for educators and researchers to consider.

### Overcoming Barriers to Adopting Peer Instruction
Accepted to ACM SIGCSE TS 2026
> [!abstract]-
> Despite decades of research on the effectiveness of Peer Instruction (PI), it can be hard to convince computing instructors to try it. In Peer Instruction an instructor displays a hard multiple-choice question during lecture, students answer individually, discuss with peers, and answer independently again. The main reasons computing instructors give for not adopting PI is a lack of awareness, concerns that it would result in less coverage of material in lecture, and the time needed to adopt it. We tried to increase awareness of PI and address instructors’ concerns during a three-day summer instructor workshop with 14 instructors in 2024. A pre-survey was administered on the first day of the workshop and a post-survey on the last day. We administered a second post-survey in the fall of 2024. Later semi-structured interviews were conducted with nine instructors. The adoption rate from the 2024 workshop was over 50%, which was much higher than the previous two workshop’s adoption rate of 23%. The workshop also increased knowledge of PI and alleviated several concerns. However, instructors wanted more follow-up support, an easier and faster way to create new PI questions, and better integration with their Learning Management System (LMS). Instructors who adopted the free tool reported positive student reactions and better understanding of student misconceptions. All instructors who adopted the free tool plan to continue to use it.

### Towards a Computer Science Topics Ontology
Accepted to ACM SIGCSE TS 2026
> [!abstract]-
> With the push towards broader computer science education, there is a never-ending need for introductory computer science resources. With the aim to help computer science educators and curriculum developers, this work shows the first steps towards a computer science topics ontology. This ontology is meant to provide a framework for CS1 vocabulary with use cases ranging from lesson repositories to a standardized CS1 topic language. Our ontology differs from other computer science education ontologies by focusing on the standards many educators must follow in their curriculum and showing the connections between those standards and the lessons they use. We show our approach using the KNARM methodology when constructing such an ontology, as well as the rationale for changes made in the process. In its current state, the ontology is queryable showing the relation between synthetic lesson data with the 2023 ACM Computer Science Curricula.

### Developing Course-Level Learning Goals for Basic Data Structures in CS2
Presented at ACM SIGCSE TS 2018

Six course-level goals identified:
- Analyze runtime efficiency of algorithms related to data structure design.
- Select appropriate abstract data types for use in a given application.
- Compare data structure tradeoffs to select the appropriate implementation for an abstract data type.

# Notes from Specifications Grading

## Learning outcomes and course design
A *learning outcome* is an observable ability or skill that a student should acquire before completion of a specified unit of study. They may be defined at the level of a single class session, a multiweek module, a semester course, or an entire degree program.

Assessments should follow directly from learning outcomes, such that a well-defined outcome typically specifies the conditions under which the assessment is performed and the standards by which it will be judged.

Fink proposes six categories of learning that can be used to specify outcomes:
1. Foundational knowledge: key concepts upon which other learning is based
2. Application: practical, critical, and creative use of knowledge
3. Integration: building connections across domains
4. Human dimensions: insight into the personal and social domain
5. Caring: motivation to learn more
6. Learning how to learn: strategies to learn more efficiently and beyond the course

*Backward design* is a process to identify and sequence learning outcomes in a course.
- First, identify the *ultimate outcomes* which represent sophisticated skills that students should acquire by the end of the course.
- Then, identify and sequence the *mediating outcomes* which are the intermediate skills that are necessary prerequisites to the ultimate outcomes.
- Finally, establish the *foundational outcomes* which are the basis upon which the others are built, and must be achieved within the first few weeks of the course.
The resulting flowchart is called an *outcomes map* and serves as the skeleton of the course design as well as a useful resource for students to complement the syllabus. Assessments and learning experiences are then judiciously attached to the skeleton to fill out the course content.

Learning experiences are typically classified as *methods* or *moves* depending on their scope.
A method is a discrete activity requiring significant time and effort to complete, such as a project or simulation, or a coherent pattern of activity that is deliberately repeated throughout the course such as peer instruction or case analysis.
Moves, on the other hand, are brief techniques (typically less than 20 minutes in duration) for exploring or elaborating on the material at hand.

## Linking grades to outcomes
If we have captured all of the intended goals of a course in its learning outcomes, and students see grades as a measure of their achievement in a course, then grades should be assigned based solely on completion of learning outcomes and nothing else. Higher grades are earned by achieving more and/or or more sophisticated learning outcomes than the minimum required to advance beyond the course.

Various cognitive models of learning provide frameworks for assessing the level of sophistication of a given outcome, the most famous of which is Bloom's taxonomy of cognitive operations published in 1956. Anderson and Krathwohl provide an updated hierarchy:
1. Remember
2. Understand
3. Apply
4. Analyze
5. Evaluate
6. Create

Perry's stage of cognitive development are more holistic, and describe various cognitive paradigms that a student progresses through linearly as they gain deeper insight into the nature of knowledge in a given field.
1. Dualism: the field is a stable collection of facts, rules, and definitions that are known by experts and should be committed to memory for recognition and recall as required by assessments.
2. Multiplicity: there are gaps in knowledge and unanswered questions, but they are generally a result of unfinished research by the true experts and should be acknowledged but avoided.
3. Relativism: the uncertainty introduced in the previous stage leads to an awareness that expert knowledge is in fact a social construction rather than natural law, but this tends to metastasize into mistrust of all expert knowledge without regard to rigor and quality of evidence.
4. Commitment: although all knowledge is constructed, some interpretations of reality are more valid or useful and therefore deserve more credence and should be relied upon unless and until a competing interpretation proves more valid.
Clearly, the more advanced operations in Bloom's hierarchy are not possible until a student has advanced to the later stages of cognitive development.

Wolcott and Lynch provide an hierarchy of problem solving performance patterns that connect the (largely unobservable) cognitive stages to specific behaviors observable during assessments.
1. Confused fact-finder: no mastery of any problem-solving skills; simply casts about looking for the one correct solution.
2. Biased jumper: adept at the skill of identifying relevant information, but only to find the most likely correct solution as quickly as possible without deeper analysis.
3. Perpetual analyzer: able to identify relevant information and interpret it in various ways without leaping to a conclusion, but fails to prioritize the proper interpretation which would lead to a satisfactory solution.
4. Pragmatic performer: adeptly identifies, interprets, and prioritizes the available information to efficiently formulate and commit to a solution, but lacks broader awareness of the limitations and possible downstream consequences of the decision.
5. Strategic re-visioner: commits to solutions tentatively, always re-evaluating in the face of new evidence and willing to change course without emotional attachment to prior decisions.

## Pass/fail grading
There is generally nothing *other* than pass/fail grading for professional certifications, which is telling. While pass/fail grading of individual courses has mixed results, we can absolutely change all assessments to pass/fail grading while still providing a letter grade for the course as a whole. The key is to make sure that the bar for passing any individual assignment is what would normally be considered a B, so that students do not coast on the bare minimum at all times.
